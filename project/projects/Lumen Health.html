Lumen Health

A quieter onboarding flow for a preventative-care clinic - designed to ask fewer questions and earn trust earlier.

00- Overview

Most preventative-care apps front-load fourteen questions before earning your trust.

Lumen is a concept I designed to explore what happens if you ask the smallest number of questions possible up front, and let the relationship deepen over the next several visits - the way a good doctor's office actually works.

The problem High onboarding drop-off in patient-facing apps
My role Research, IA, UX, visual design
Outcome Concept prototype + clickable Figma
01- The Brief

What I set out to do, and what I deliberately ignored.

I framed the project around one question: how few questions can an onboarding flow ask before it stops feeling impersonal? I deliberately ignored anything to do with billing, scheduling or scope creep into a "full app" - that would have been a six-month project, not a four-week one.

This is an honest constraint, not a hand-wave. A real version of this product would need all of those flows. I'm a beginner - narrowing scope is how I make sure the work I do show is actually finished.

02- Research

I started by writing down every onboarding flow I'd ever quit halfway through.

I logged eight first-run experiences across health and wellness apps, screenshotted each screen, and annotated the moments I felt over-asked or under-explained. Three patterns kept showing up:

03- Process

Three sketch rounds, two dead ends, one direction I kept.

I started with index cards - one screen per card - and reshuffled the deck until the order felt like a conversation. The first two attempts were too clever (one used a chat metaphor that collapsed on the third question). The third was almost embarrassingly plain. That was the one.

Attempt 02Card stack - felt like a quiz, not a clinic.

04- Key Decisions

Three calls that shaped the flow.

  1. One question per screen.

    Counterintuitive for an app that's trying to be efficient, but each screen becomes legible at a glance - and the user can pause anywhere without losing context.

  2. Defer the medical history.

    Moved the long medical form from screen 02 to "after your first visit." Onboarding only asks: your name, your reason for being here, and a phone number.

  3. Always offer "I'd rather tell my doctor in person."

    An off-ramp on every sensitive question. The product earns trust by not insisting.

05- Final Design

The flow, end-to-end.

Six screens, plain typography, one decision at a time. Animations are slow on purpose - they give the user a beat to read what they're agreeing to.

06- What I Learned

The two things I'd do differently.

I'd test with real users. This is a concept - no patients ever saw it. The patterns I found in the audit are real, but my solution is a hypothesis until someone other than me tries it.

I'd commit to one font earlier. I burned two days on type comparisons that didn't change anything meaningful. Next time: pick something defensible, ship, revisit at the end.
